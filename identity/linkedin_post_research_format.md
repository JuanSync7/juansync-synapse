Most teams running iterative AI experiments keep terrible records.

You know the pattern: a shared doc with "v2 was better I think?" and a Slack thread nobody can find two weeks later. When something regresses, nobody knows why — because nobody wrote down what they expected *before* they made the change.

We built a simple format to fix this. Two files, auto-generated by the research agent:

**iterations.tsv** — a tab-separated log where every experiment gets a row. The key insight: each row captures both a *hypothesis* (what you expected before editing) and a *change_summary* (what actually happened after scoring). Keeping these separate is the whole point. When your hypotheses systematically diverge from outcomes — say, you keep predicting that simplification will help but it regresses 4 out of 5 times — the log makes that pattern visible.

**changelog.md** — a human-readable narrative written at the end of a run. What improved. What didn't work. Where your predictions were wrong. Remaining gaps. It's the document you hand to someone joining the project so they don't repeat your mistakes.

The format supports two scoring modes: numerical (N/M criteria passed) and comparative (A/B judging across ranked dimensions). Both use git diff between commits rather than file snapshots, keeping everything lightweight and version-controlled.

A few things we learned building this:

The "hypothesis mispredictions" section is the most valuable part. It forces you to confront patterns in your own reasoning — not just what worked, but *why you kept guessing wrong*.

The agent creates the research/ directory itself on iteration 001. Pre-creating it was a common source of subtle bugs, so we made it a rule: don't touch it, let the agent bootstrap.

Three-digit zero-padded iteration numbers. Sounds trivial, but it keeps everything sorted correctly when you have 100+ experiments.

If you're running automated prompt research, structured experiment logging changes how you think about the work. It stops being "try stuff and hope" and starts being science.
